================================================================
MEERA PILLAI (FOUNDER, SKINSTINCT) - VOICE & WRITING STYLE GUIDE
Derived from 15 published pieces: 4 LinkedIn posts, 11 newsletters
================================================================


----------------------------------------------------------------
0. THE VOICE IN ONE PARAGRAPH (use this as a quick brief)
----------------------------------------------------------------
Meera writes like a former pharma formulation scientist explaining
skincare chemistry to an intelligent friend. She is precise, calm,
evidence-first and quietly sceptical of marketing language. She
leads with a concrete fact, number or moment, explains the
mechanism plainly, draws careful boundaries around her own claims
("I'm not saying X. I'm saying Y."), admits her own mistakes and
limits without drama, discloses her commercial stake, and ends by
telling the reader what question to ask - of any brand, not just
hers. She never hypes, never sells hard, never uses exclamation
marks, and would rather sound unflattering and accurate than
impressive and vague.


----------------------------------------------------------------
1. WHO SHE IS ON THE PAGE (IDENTITY & POSITIONING)
----------------------------------------------------------------
- Former pharmaceutical formulation professional (2 years), now
  founder. Credibility comes from process knowledge: CoAs, pH,
  stability testing, batch sampling, contract manufacturers.
- Explicitly NOT a dermatologist, no medical degree - and she says
  so. Her authority is bounded and she states the boundary.
- Positions herself as a translator of the "gap" between label
  claims and formulation reality. "The gap" is her core theme.
- An insider who critiques the system, not individuals. She calls
  out industry practices while being fair to the people in them.
- India-aware: humidity, Indian cities (Mumbai, Chennai, Kochi,
  Kolkata), Indian UV levels, Indian cosmetic regulation.
- A founder who treats her brand as secondary to the information.
  Skinstinct appears as a worked example, never as the hero.


----------------------------------------------------------------
2. CORE BELIEFS THAT DRIVE EVERY PIECE
----------------------------------------------------------------
1. The label is the beginning of the question, not the answer.
2. Formulation (pH, base/vehicle, concentration, stability, batch
   process) matters more than the ingredient list.
3. Documentation is the proof. If it isn't documented, it's a claim.
4. Honest-but-unflattering beats impressive-but-vague.
5. Readers are capable of understanding the science if it's
   explained plainly.
6. Readers should be empowered to ask questions of ANY brand.
7. Binary framings (natural vs synthetic, clean vs toxic) are
   unproductive. The truth is "more specific."
8. Gaps in the industry are mostly systemic - carelessness,
   commercial pressure, inherited protocols - not malice.


----------------------------------------------------------------
3. TONE & EMOTIONAL REGISTER
----------------------------------------------------------------
- Measured, calm, direct. Confident without being loud.
- Emotion is present but understated and named precisely:
  "the specific discomfort of sitting in that room",
  "the thing we got wrong that I've been sitting with",
  "I find it genuinely interesting to write about".
- Dry, low-key wit, never jokes for their own sake:
  "She didn't, which is a reasonable outcome because..."
  "longer than I planned and shorter than it apparently needed"
  "A moisturiser formulated for Oslo in February... Chennai in August"
- Self-deprecating about the brand's origin story:
  "It is not a particularly inspiring story."
- Mildly frustrated at the industry, but frustration is expressed
  through flat understatement, not outrage:
  "This is legal. It is also not helpful."
- Warm towards readers, especially in newsletters - thanks them
  sincerely and specifically ("I mean that specifically, not as a
  sign-off").


----------------------------------------------------------------
4. STRUCTURE OF A TYPICAL PIECE
----------------------------------------------------------------
Almost every piece follows this arc:

  (1) HOOK - a concrete, specific entry point:
      - a number/data point ("In the 12 months to June 2025, 23%...")
      - a scene with a date and place ("In 2021 I was sitting in a
        stability review meeting...", "Last September I was at a
        trade fair in Mumbai.")
      - a claim that quietly challenges the reader's assumption
        ("The niacinamide serum you're using probably has...")
      - a real customer question ("A customer wrote to me last
        month asking...")
      - a meta-statement about the email itself (newsletters):
        "I want to talk about...", "I've been avoiding writing this
        one because...", "I almost didn't write this email..."

  (2) STATE THE GAP - what people believe vs what's actually true.
      Often: "This is true. It is also almost entirely useless..."

  (3) MECHANISM - explain the science step by step, in plain words,
      frequently organised as a set of conditions/factors
      ("three other things", "All three of those conditions have
      to be met", "The second thing...", "The third thing...",
      "Take penetration... Then there's concentration... Finally,
      stability.")

  (4) EVIDENCE / OWN EXAMPLE - specific numbers from research or
      from Skinstinct's own testing, returns data, reformulation.

  (5) THE BOUNDARY - she explicitly limits her claim:
      "I am not making a case that niacinamide doesn't work."
      "I want to be precise about what I'm not saying here."
      "I'm not trying to make you distrust every product..."
      "I want to be careful here because..."

  (6) PRACTICAL TAKEAWAY - what the reader should do or ask.
      Usually: ask the brand in writing for specific data.

  (7) CLOSING - one of:
      - the "that's useful information" line: "If the answer is
        vague or doesn't arrive, that is also useful information."
      - a transparency statement about Skinstinct ("We publish that
        information for our products because it exists...")
      - a forward promise ("When we get it right, I'll tell you
        exactly what we did and why.")
      - newsletters end with just: "Meera"


----------------------------------------------------------------
5. SIGNATURE RHETORICAL MOVES (the most recognisable tells)
----------------------------------------------------------------
A. The "It is also" deflation
   A short factual sentence, followed by a flat qualifier.
   "Most serums don't list their pH on the label. This is legal.
    It is also not helpful."
   "This is true. It is also almost entirely useless information..."

B. The "I'm not saying X. I'm saying Y." boundary
   Used in almost every piece. Pre-empts misreading, shows fairness.
   "I'm not saying natural ingredients are better or worse than
    synthetic ones... What I'm saying is..."

C. The reframe: "not A, it's B" / "X is the beginning, not the end"
   "The base isn't decoration around the active. It determines
    whether the active arrives."
   "'clean' is a marketing category, not a scientific one"
   "The concentration on the label reflects the start of the
    product's life, not the end of it."

D. Silence/absence as information
   "If the answer is that they don't have that documentation,
    that's useful information."
   "...that tells you something useful about how closely the
    formulation chemistry is being managed."

E. Stake disclosure
   "We don't currently sell a peptide product. I'm telling you this
    because... I don't have a commercial stake in how you read it."
   "I'm not selling a sunscreen. I want to be clear about that."

F. Owning mistakes plainly
   "it should have been right the first time"
   "it took us embarrassingly long to make the connection"
   "by us in our early formulations"
   "having a pharmaceutical background doesn't make you immune..."

G. "Not from us specifically"
   She redirects the takeaway to the whole market, not her brand.
   "You should ask for them. Not from us specifically - from any
    brand whose products you're spending significant money on."

H. Contrast pairs that make abstraction concrete
   "a 500-person double-blind study or 12 people rating how their
    skin felt after two weeks"
   "Seven peptides at sub-therapeutic concentrations is not better
    than two peptides at effective concentrations."
   '"Contains niacinamide" tells you almost nothing. "Contains
    niacinamide at 4%, pH 5.5-6.0... " tells you something.'

I. Fair steelman of the other side
   Acknowledges legitimate reasons before disagreeing:
   "These are real and legitimate commercial considerations. I made
    a different choice because..."
   "What clean beauty gets right:..."


----------------------------------------------------------------
6. SENTENCE-LEVEL STYLE
----------------------------------------------------------------
- Rhythm: medium-to-long explanatory sentences punctuated by short,
  blunt ones (3-8 words) for emphasis. The short sentence usually
  lands the point.
    "Niacin is not inert."
    "The label percentage stayed the same the entire time."
    "It was designed for the wrong context."
    "The sensitisation is permanent."
- Heavy use of first person singular ("I want", "I think", "I've
  seen") and "we" for the company. Second person "you" to address
  the reader directly.
- Frequent openers: "I want to...", "Here's why it matters.",
  "Here's what I want you to take from this.", "The short version...
  The longer version.", "Start here:", "Take X."
- Contractions used naturally (I'm, don't, it's, you're), but she
  switches to full forms ("It is", "I am not") for emphasis and
  formality at key moments.
- Dashes written as spaced hyphens " - " for asides and
  clarifications. Uses these a lot.
- Parenthetical clarifications for technical terms:
  "The skin barrier - technically the stratum corneum - is..."
  "(participants rating their own skin feel)"
- Lists are kept INSIDE prose, comma-separated, never bulleted:
  "ascorbic acid, sodium ascorbyl phosphate, magnesium ascorbyl
   phosphate, ascorbyl glucoside, ethyl ascorbic acid, or several
   others."
- Hedging words are precise, not vague: "approximately", "around",
  "roughly", "estimated at somewhere between 1-3%", "starts at
  around 2%". She hedges because the data is a range, not to avoid
  commitment.
- Scare quotes around marketing terms she's examining:
  "natural", "clean", "clinically tested", "barrier repair",
  "skin-loving", "multi-peptide complex".
- British/Indian English spelling: oxidise, sensitisation,
  moisturiser, colour, standardised, oestrogenic, formulation,
  characterised, organisation.


----------------------------------------------------------------
7. VOCABULARY
----------------------------------------------------------------
Words and phrases she reaches for repeatedly:
  honest / honestly / "I want to be honest about something"
  specific / specifically / "the truth is more specific"
  genuinely, meaningful, properly, plainly, directly, precisely
  "useful information", "worth investigating", "worth doing"
  "the gap", "documentation", "the data", "the evidence"
  "the actual evidence shows", "the research supports"
  formulation, base, vehicle, delivery, concentration, stability,
  pH, CoA, batch, INCI, contract manufacturer, bioavailable
  "in practice", "in skin terms", "the practical implication"

Technical terms are used correctly and then explained in plain
words in the same or next sentence ("It causes vasodilation. In
skin terms, that's the flushing reaction...").

Words she NEVER uses (avoid entirely):
  game-changer, holy grail, miracle, glow up, obsessed, must-have,
  revolutionary, clean (as praise), toxic (as fear), chemical-free,
  magic, hero ingredient, skin-loving (except to critique it),
  "you deserve", "treat yourself", limited time, hurry, amazing,
  incredible, luxe, secret.


----------------------------------------------------------------
8. USE OF NUMBERS & EVIDENCE
----------------------------------------------------------------
- Numbers are her main persuasion tool. Almost every paragraph has
  one: pH values (3.2, 3.8, 5.5-5.8), concentrations (2%, 4-5%,
  10%), time (8-10 months, 14 months, 20 minutes), sample sizes
  (25 people, 500-person), business metrics (23% returns, 71%, 8%,
  67% repeat rate vs 20-40% industry).
- Numbers are specific, not rounded for effect, and ranges are
  given when the evidence is a range.
- Cites study type and source where relevant ("A 2022 study from
  the University of Liverpool...", "in-vitro", "double-blind",
  "consumer perception study").
- Distinguishes quality of evidence: in-vitro vs clinical,
  supplier-run vs independent, mechanism vs product efficacy.
- Uses her own company data openly, including unflattering data.
- Admits uncertainty in interpretation: "I don't know how to
  separate 'the formulation works' from 'the communication builds
  trust' in that number, and probably both are contributing."


----------------------------------------------------------------
9. HOW SHE TALKS ABOUT SKINSTINCT
----------------------------------------------------------------
- As a worked example, placed late in the piece, never the lead.
- Always with specifics: "pH 5.5-5.8 and we document it on every
  batch", "mid-batch CoA sampling", "stability testing at
  three-month intervals".
- Frames the brand's rigour as cost and inconvenience, not glamour:
  "adds to our timelines and costs more than the industry standard"
- Openly states what the brand does NOT do or sell yet, and what
  it won't claim until it has data: "When we have the formulation
  data to support that claim specifically, we will."
- Never uses discounts, urgency, launches as hooks, or CTAs to buy.
- "this list is not going to be a place where I sell you things."


----------------------------------------------------------------
10. HOW SHE TALKS ABOUT THE INDUSTRY & OTHERS
----------------------------------------------------------------
- Critiques practices, labels and systems - not named competitors
  or individuals.
- Explicitly protects people from blame: "I'm not saying the people
  in that room were incompetent or malicious. I'm saying the system
  had a gap..."
- Explains WHY the gap exists (cost, complexity, commercial
  pressure, regulation not requiring it, customers not asking).
- Notes what is legal vs what is helpful - a recurring distinction.
- Gives credit where due: "Some brands do robust independent
  testing." "What clean beauty gets right..."


----------------------------------------------------------------
11. PLATFORM DIFFERENCES
----------------------------------------------------------------
LINKEDIN POSTS (~450-650 words)
- No greeting, no sign-off, no hashtags, no emojis.
- Opens cold with a data point, a dated scene, or a direct claim.
- Paragraphs are full prose (3-6 sentences) - she does NOT use the
  one-line-per-paragraph LinkedIn format.
- Audience is broader: customers AND industry peers/founders.
  Often includes advice for people building brands ("If you're
  building a skincare brand for the Indian market, the first
  question to ask your contract manufacturer is...").
- Slightly more formal and more "industry commentary."
- Categories seen: Ingredient Deep-Dive, Founder Story, India-
  Specific Context, Industry Transparency.

EMAIL NEWSLETTERS (~450-700 words)
- Starts with "Hi," on its own line. Ends with "Meera" on its own
  line. No P.S., no links-heavy CTA, no "Cheers/Best/xx".
- First line is often meta and personal - about writing the email:
  "I want to start with something direct",
  "I've been avoiding writing this one because...",
  "I want to be careful writing this one because..."
- More intimate, more "I", more reader questions and customer
  anecdotes. Occasionally teases the series ("More on specific
  actives in the next few emails.").
- Subject lines: plain, candid, often with a colon or a
  parenthetical aside, sometimes a mild promise of honesty:
    "What you're actually signed up for"
    "pH - the question nobody puts on the label"
    "The Vitamin C email (I've been putting this off)"
    "What an 'active' actually does (and why most explanations
     stop too early)"
    "Clean beauty gets some things right. The rest is making
     things harder."
    "18 months of Skinstinct. Some data, some honesty."
  Pattern: no clickbait, no emojis, no caps; favours words like
  "actually", "honest(y)", "what the research says".
- Categories seen: Brand Philosophy, Formulation Science,
  Ingredient Deep-Dive, Consumer Education, Industry Transparency,
  Founder Story.


----------------------------------------------------------------
12. FORMATTING RULES
----------------------------------------------------------------
- Prose only. No bullets, no numbered lists, no bold, no headers
  inside the body.
- No exclamation marks. Anywhere.
- No emojis, no hashtags.
- Spaced hyphen " - " as the dash.
- Uses quotation marks for marketing terms and customer quotes.
- Occasional ALL CAPS only when reproducing a real banner/label.
- Paragraphs of consistent medium length; one short standalone
  line allowed for emphasis ("The longer version.").


----------------------------------------------------------------
13. DO / DON'T QUICK REFERENCE
----------------------------------------------------------------
DO
- Open with a specific number, date, place or real question.
- Explain the mechanism in plain language.
- Break complexity into 2-3 named conditions or factors.
- Say what you are NOT claiming.
- Admit mistakes and limits of your expertise.
- Disclose whether you sell the product being discussed.
- Give the reader a question to ask any brand.
- Treat missing information as information.
- Be fair to the opposing view before disagreeing.
- Stay understated; let the facts carry the weight.

DON'T
- Hype, exaggerate, or use superlatives.
- Fearmonger or use "toxic/chemical" framing.
- Make medical claims or speak as a dermatologist.
- Blame individuals or name-and-shame competitors.
- Put the brand at the centre of the story.
- Use sales CTAs, urgency, discounts or "link in bio".
- Use bullet points, emojis, hashtags or exclamation marks.
- Round numbers for drama or cite vague "studies show".
- Turn the founder story into a hero narrative.


----------------------------------------------------------------
14. BEFORE / AFTER EXAMPLE
----------------------------------------------------------------
GENERIC SKINCARE COPY (not Meera):
  "Our hero Vitamin C serum is a total game-changer! Clinically
   tested and packed with 15% Vitamin C for that radiant glow.
   Grab yours before it sells out!"

IN MEERA'S VOICE:
  "A Vitamin C serum that lists 15% on the label is telling you how
   much went in on the day it was made. It isn't telling you how
   much is left after six weeks on a bathroom shelf in Chennai.
   L-ascorbic acid oxidises with air, light and heat, and without
   airless packaging, a pH below 3.5 and an antioxidant support
   system, the concentration drops measurably within months. I'm
   not saying Vitamin C doesn't work - the evidence for it is
   strong. I'm saying the percentage is the start of the question.
   If the brand can't tell you how they tested stability, that's
   useful information."


----------------------------------------------------------------
15. SELF-CHECK BEFORE PUBLISHING IN HER VOICE
----------------------------------------------------------------
[ ] Does it open with something concrete (number, scene, question)?
[ ] Is there a clear "gap" between claim and reality?
[ ] Is the mechanism explained plainly, with at least one number?
[ ] Is there an explicit "I'm not saying..." boundary?
[ ] Is any Skinstinct mention late, specific and non-salesy?
[ ] Is the commercial stake disclosed where relevant?
[ ] Does it end with a question the reader can ask any brand, a
    transparency note, or a quiet forward promise?
[ ] Zero exclamation marks, emojis, hashtags, bullets, hype words?
[ ] British spellings (oxidise, moisturiser, colour)?
[ ] Would it still be true and useful if Skinstinct didn't exist?
